How to use this page

Today is ten short experiments. In each one you give the AI a prompt, change one thing, and watch what it does. Then we look at why it behaves that way.

From asking to directing

You already brief consultants, staff and committees; AI needs the same discipline: a clear brief, your context and your review, because unlike a person it rarely says ‘I don’t know’ and may invent details.

The rhythm of every activity

Do it→Take-away→Why it works→Next activity

The rules for every activity

  1. Use Copilot Chat with the Work badge showing (university account). Gemini works too.
  2. Start a new chat for each run, so the AI has no memory of the last one.
  3. Prompt and data in one go: copy the whole box, paste it, send once. The AI cannot see the slide.
  4. Synthetic data only. Every rule, table and email here is invented for practice. Never paste real student or staff data.

Each activity has a closed section, “What it showed”. Open it after the activity, not before: the surprise is part of the learning.

Ask the AI the same question twice

The situation: A colleague needs one figure for a report: how many Indian students use AI for coursework. Everyone asks the AI in two new chats and compares the two numbers.

1We are testing

Does the AI give the same number to the same question?

2The one difference

There is none: you send the same prompt in two fresh chats.

3Look for

Do your two numbers match?

Steps

  2. Copy the prompt below, paste it and send. Write down the number.
  3. Open a second new chat, paste the same prompt, send. Write down that number.

Activity 1Why it works · slide 5

Why the answer changes: the model predicts one word at a time

An AI chatbot does not look up an answer. It writes one, a word at a time.

  1. It reads your prompt and everything written so far.
  2. It gives every possible next word a score.
  3. It picks one word, often the top one, sometimes another.
  4. It adds that word and repeats, until the answer is complete.

Example · after “The committee recommends that the university …”

review38%
expand24%
postpone14%
invest9%
consult6%

Scores are illustrative, not from a real model. Run it again and “expand” may win instead of “review”, and the whole answer goes another way.

What it means for you

Ask the same question twice and you may get two different answers. Treat one run as a draft, not the answer, and check anything you will rely on.

Activity 2Using it safely · slide 9

Use AI where errors are cheap and easy to check

Two questions decide where AI belongs in your work: what does an error cost, and how easily can you check the output?

Put the two questions together and every task falls into one of four zones. The table shows each zone, what to do in it, and examples from university work.

ZoneCost of an error · ease of checkingHow to use AI hereExamples
Use freelyLow cost, easy to checkLet AI draft, and skim the draft before you send it.Meeting summary; draft of a routine email; ideas for an event theme
Use, then verifyHigh cost, easy to checkAI drafts, and a named person checks every fact and figure.First draft of a circular; figures for a board note; FAQ from a published policy
Use with careLow cost, hard to checkAI is good for ideas here. Do not rely on anything you cannot check.Literature scan for ideas; translating a public notice; summary of a long report
A human decidesHigh cost, hard to checkAI may support the research, but a person decides and signs.Admission decisions; exam marking; regulatory submissions; grievance outcomes

Decisions about people, marks and regulators are never handed to a model. Most of the real value sits in the two “easy to check” zones, where checking costs little.

What it means for you

Pick one task from your own week and place it in a zone. If it lands in “A human decides”, AI can help you prepare, but the decision and the signature stay with a person.

Activity 2Using it safely · slide 10

Some material may go into an AI tool, and some may not

Sort a document before you paste it. If you would not email it to an outside consultant, it does not go into an AI tool.

Most of us already paste things into chatbots: minutes, drafts, and sometimes student data. The question is not whether to use AI, but what may go in, and into which tool.

Kind of materialWhich tool it may go intoExamples
Public informationAny tool, including public ones.Prospectus; press release; public regulations; published papers; event notices
Internal materialOnly the tool the university has approved.Committee minutes; draft policies; internal budgets; anonymised data; process manuals
Personal or confidentialOnly systems approved for this kind of data.Student records; patient data; unreleased exams; staff appraisals; unpublished research

The consultant test

Ask yourself whether you would email this document to an outside consultant who has not signed a confidentiality agreement. If you would not, it does not go into an unapproved tool.

The law in India

Under the Digital Personal Data Protection (DPDP) Act, 2023, the university stays responsible for any personal data it passes to a service, and AI tools are included.

The middle row only works if people know which tool is approved. In the session, few people knew. Find out which AI tool the university has approved and where the approved list is published.

What it means for you

Think of the last document you pasted into a chatbot and decide which row it belonged to. Before you paste the next one, sort it first, and use the approved tool for anything internal.

Activity 3Why it works · slide 12

Fluent does not mean correct: four real cases show what happens

Each of these cases began as a confident, well-written AI output.

YearCaseWhat happenedThe lesson
2023Mata v. Avianca · USALawyers were fined over case law the AI invented. A court filing cited six decisions that did not exist, and the judge fined the lawyers and their firm $5,000.Check every citation.
2024Moffatt v. Air Canada · CanadaThe airline was held liable for its chatbot’s advice. The chatbot invented a bereavement-fare refund rule, and a tribunal ordered the airline to pay up.You own what your AI says.
2025Deloitte · AustraliaDeloitte gave a partial refund on a government report. The report, written for a federal department, contained fabricated references and a made-up court quote.Big names are not immune.
2023Samsung · South KoreaEngineers pasted confidential source code and meeting notes into a public chatbot, and the company restricted its use.Pasted data can leak.

The common thread is not bad technology. In every case, unchecked output reached a decision or the public.

Your signature

The AI does not sign the report. You do. So follow four habits:

  1. Treat AI output as a draft or an aid to analysis.
  2. Check consequential facts against trusted sources.
  3. Ask the AI for its assumptions, its uncertainty and its evidence.
  4. Keep confidential data out of unapproved tools.

What it means for you

Accountability stays with whoever signs. Before your name goes on AI-assisted work, check the citations and figures yourself.

What it showed (open after the activity)

A fluent analysis does not always survive the question. In our rehearsal, Prompt 1 produced a briefing with three findings, a change table, a table of possible causes and three actions. Every change it calculated was right (−2, −17, −1 and +2 points).

But its heading read “Communication Skills improved but remains lowest”. That is wrong: in October, Maths III (61%) is lowest, and the briefing’s own body even calls Communication Skills “second-lowest”. It also said “Most technical courses remained stable”, leaving Maths III out of “technical” with no basis. Its actions, such as “Identify students with repeated absences”, assume data we never gave it.

Prompt 2 gave a correct change table and one summary line, then wrote: “No information is provided on class schedules, assessments, holidays, student numbers, faculty changes, or attendance records by section, so no causes can be determined from this table alone.” It gave no causes, no targets and no actions. The brief made it stop where the data stops.

Your own outputs may differ from these: each run is a sample. Whatever you got, any sentence that is not a number from the table (a “stable”, a possible cause, an action) should face the question “How do you know?”.

Take-away

Activity 4Why it works · slide 14

The five checks make “How do you know?” systematic

Score every AI output on five checks before it carries your name. You will use this card in four activities today.

Each check has a question and a quick test. The test is the part to remember: it is what you actually do.

CheckThe questionThe test
A · AccuracyCan every fact and figure be verified?Check two figures against the source.
R · RelevanceDoes it answer the task you set?Re-read the line of your prompt that states the task.
C · CompletenessIs anything important missing?Ask what the reader will ask next.
T · TraceabilityCan each claim be traced to a source?Point to the line, page or clause it comes from.
U · UsabilityCan the reader act on it as it is?Ask whether you would send it today.

How to score

Give each check one of three marks: ✓ means it passes, ✗ means it fails, and ? means you cannot tell. A ? on Accuracy or Traceability counts as a fail, because a claim you cannot verify or trace is not safe to sign.

The “?” rule is a house rule we suggest, not a published standard.

Where you use the card today

  • Activity 4 · How do you know?
  • Activity 5 · Fix the brief
  • Activity 8 · Three parents
  • Activity 10 · Your Monday brief

The same card is on the printed 6C card, so you can score at your desk.

What it means for you

Ask yourself which of the five checks you skip most often. For most people it is Traceability, so make pointing to the source a habit.

Activity 4Why it works · slide 16

How much checking a piece of work needs rises with the stakes

You need more of the five checks (Accuracy, Relevance, Completeness, Traceability, Usability) as the stakes rise.

The practical question for a head of department is not “should we check?” but “how much checking does this piece of work deserve, and who has to sign it?”. The answer climbs in three steps: more checks and more reviewers as the stakes rise.

StakesKind of workWho reviewsChecks from the card
LowInternal drafts: your own notes, first drafts, brainstorming.You.2 of 5: Relevance and Usability. A quick read: does it answer the task, and can you use it as it is?
MediumExternal communication: circulars, parent emails, website and press text.You, plus a colleague who confirms the facts.4 of 5: Accuracy, Relevance, Completeness and Usability. Every fact and figure is checked, and a colleague confirms the facts before it goes out.
HighBoard, regulatory and student-outcome work: board papers, accreditation filings, anything on a student’s record.You, plus a second reviewer, plus a named approver who signs.All 5, with each claim traced to its source. The named approver reads it and signs.

Where today’s activities sit

  • Low stakes: Activity 10, your Monday brief, is an internal draft.
  • Medium stakes: Activity 8, the reply to a parent, is external communication.
  • High stakes: the optional board note. Score it on all five checks.

The assignment of checks to each step is a house rule we suggest, not a published standard.

What it means for you

Decide which step most of your office’s AI-assisted work sits on, and name the person who signs at the top step.

A good brief contains six things, which we call the 6C

These are the six things you would tell a capable new senior colleague before handing over a task.

Think of a prompt as delegation, not as a technique. Leaders already brief people this way. In Activity 5, the five lines you added covered three of these parts: Composition (who reads it and in what shape), Constraints (keep every number, add nothing) and Critique (list what is ambiguous).

PartNameWhat it tells the AI
C1CharacterIt sets the perspective you want, such as analyst, reviewer or planner.
C2ContextIt gives the background, the audience and the actual data.
C3CommandIt states one clear task as an action.
C4Constraints (often missing)It sets the scope, the limits and what not to do.
C5CompositionIt fixes the format, length and structure of the answer.
C6Critique (often missing)It tells the answer how to check itself before it is finished.

The two parts most often missing

In most prompts people write, Constraints and Critique are left out. They are exactly the parts that stop the AI from guessing confidently.

How many parts does a task need?

Not every task needs all six. A quick question needs two: the Command and the Context. Anything that feeds a decision, such as a decision document, needs all six.

What it means for you

Before you send a prompt that will feed a decision, check that it says what not to do (Constraints) and how to check the answer (Critique).

Activity 8Why it works · slide 27

Give the AI a checklist, not “make it better”

This is called self-review: the AI checks its own draft against a checklist you give it. In Activity 8, the checklist had seven points.

The point in one sentence: do not ask the AI to “make it better”. Give it a checklist, ask it to list the problems first, then fix them, and read the result yourself.

What most people do, and why it fails

Most people ask “make it better”. The wording gets smoother, but the hostel reply from Activity 8 still promises ₹45,000 in 7 days. Nothing wrong gets fixed, because the AI was never told what “wrong” means.

What to do instead: four steps, on the same draft

  1. The draft. The reply from Activity 8 promises the full deposit of ₹45,000 within 7 days.
  2. The seven checks. Accuracy, audience fit, commitments, policy, tone, length, and whether it answers what the reader asked. They find three problems: the amount is wrong (the policy gives ₹40,000 less a ₹2,000 fee, so ₹38,000); “7 days” breaks the policy, which says 30 working days; and, for Card C’s parent, the reply ignores the complaint.
  3. The fixed reply, with a list of what changed. For Card C’s parent: “We are sorry your daughter was treated rudely on 5 May, and we are looking into it. The refund is ₹38,000, within 30 working days. …” (illustrative).
  4. You still read it. A fixed reply can add new mistakes, and your name goes on it.

The seventh check, “does it answer what the reader asked?”, is the one only you can judge, because only you know the reader. It is why the three parents got three different replies.

Self-review prompt (add your draft, and the policy if there is one)
Review this draft against seven checks: accuracy, audience fit, commitments, policy, tone, length, and whether it answers what the reader asked. List the three most important problems, then revise it and list what you changed.

Where to use it

Use it for anything people will rely on: letters, circulars, notices, regulator replies, minutes, policy drafts, reports and proposals. Skip it for brainstorming, rough notes and anything where a mistake costs little.

What it means for you

Never send “make it better”. Send a checklist, ask for the problems first, and read the fixed version before it goes out.

Two ideas explain how the model produces an answer

These pages explain attention, and how the model reads text in tokens within a limited context.

A simplified transformer view: attention is a mechanism, not understanding

Inside the model, attention lets each word weigh every other word in the sentence. In the sentence “The committee approved the budget because it was within limits”, the model has to work out which word “it” means. The illustrative weights below show where the attention from “it” goes.

budget62%
committee18%
limits8%
approved5%

The text passes through four steps: the input is split into tokens, the tokens become embeddings with their positions, many attention layers process them, and the model produces probabilities for the next token.

Input tokens→Embeddings + position→Attention layers (×N)→Next-token probabilities

The weights are learned from patterns in training text. The model resolves “it” to “budget” statistically; nobody told it what a budget is. This pattern-matching is why it can be confidently wrong.

Tokens, context and embeddings: the model reads in pieces, holds only so much, and turns meaning into numbers

Tokens. A token is a chunk of text, not always a whole word: “Prompting is a skill.” might be split as Prompt ing ␣is ␣a ␣skill . (␣ marks a space). The same sentence can take very different numbers of tokens in different languages. In one illustrative split, “The meeting has been postponed to Monday.” took about 8 tokens in English, about 14 in Hindi and about 18 in Kannada; exact counts vary by model.

Context window. The context window is what the model can see right now: your brief, an attached file and your new message. As a chat grows, older turns drop out. One page is roughly 600–700 tokens, and window sizes range from tens of thousands to over a million tokens, depending on the tool.

Embeddings. The model turns meaning into numbers, and similar meanings get similar numbers, so they sit close together. “Dean”, “registrar” and “principal” sit near each other; so do “fees”, “budget” and “grant”. This is why the model finds related ideas even when the words differ.

CostPaid tools bill per token, so long files cost more.
Long documentsA long document may not fit. Ask what was read, or split it.
Indian languagesIndian languages often need more tokens, so less fits in one prompt.

You can see this yourself: paste the same sentence in English and in Kannada into a public tokenizer and compare the counts.

When AI starts to act for you, add approval gates

This page explains what changes when AI moves from drafting text to taking actions for you, and the gates to set before it does.

From answering to acting: more autonomy needs more approval gates

AI tools are moving from drafting text to taking actions on your behalf. At each step up, the AI does more on its own, so the organisation needs more points where a person approves before it continues.

LevelWhat it doesYour role
ChatIt answers and drafts.You copy, check and act.
AssistantIt works in your files, mail and calendar, for example drafting replies in your inbox.You approve each output.
AgentIt browses, fills forms and runs multi-step tasks, for example booking rooms or submitting requests.It acts alone between the approval gates you set.

Set these four gates before any agent goes live

  1. Start read-only: let it look before it can change anything.
  2. Approve before acting: require sign-off before anything is sent, paid or deleted.
  3. Keep an activity log: record every action so it can be reviewed.
  4. Name an owner: one person answers for what it does.

These gates apply to any agent a vendor offers, whatever the product.

Checked stages, one job each

The situation: The IQAC office is drafting the self-study report for accreditation, with dozens of criteria that each need data and evidence. One prompt gives numbers nobody can trace, so the work is split into checked stages.

The IQAC office is drafting the self-study report for accreditation: dozens of criteria, each needing data, evidence and a narrative. Asked in one prompt, AI returns a fluent report whose numbers nobody can trace, and the error is found, if at all, by the committee. Instead, use one prompt per stage, a written hand-off, a check at every step, and a named person at the end.

StagePrompt (shortened)Hand-offCheck before moving on

2 · Tabulate“Build the data table for criterion 1 only, from this extract.”Draft tableIs there a source for each row?

Role prompting is useful, not magical

A role sets perspective, vocabulary and priorities, as in “Act as a senior facilities planner.” It does not grant credentials, access or guaranteed expertise. Avoid inflated titles and give precise instructions instead.

The situation: The estates committee must decide on an 84-page campus solar tender. The AI extracts the claims and their evidence, tests them against our criteria and exposes the gaps before anyone decides.

A summary is not a decision. For a long document that feeds a decision, work through six steps. The example is an illustrative 84-page tender for campus solar power.

  2. Claims and evidence: for example, “Payback 5.8 years” is a vendor estimate, on page 41.
